# Engineering Ethics: Boeing’s 737 MAX Crisis

The Boeing 737 MAX crisis revolves around the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) which resulted in two fatal crashes in 2018 and 2019. It makes this one of the most consequential engineering ethics failures. This situation shows how competitive pressure, flawed organizational culture, regulatory shortcomings, and ethical lapses can produce catastrophic outcomes. Evaluating this crisis through the lens of engineering ethics such as through the ASME Canons, reveals how Boeing, the FAA, airlines, customers, employees, and investors shaped or were affected by decision-making that ultimately jeopardized public safety.

---

## Competitive Pressure and the Introduction of MCAS

MCAS was introduced primarily to counteract aerodynamic tendencies resulting from Boeing’s decision to mount larger, fuel-efficient engines on the existing 737 airframe. Airbus had recently released the A320neo, and Boeing felt commercial pressure to deliver a competing aircraft quickly. 

Rather than redesigning the airframe, Boeing engineers modified the existing 737 platform, by intervening with software to maintain handling characteristics. A key ethical concern emerged when Boeing minimized MCAS’s visibility to pilots and airlines to avoid additional simulator training. Training requirements would increase costs for airlines, delay aircraft deliveries, and potentially undermine Boeing’s competitive edge. 

Engineering ethics principles, especially the ASME Canon, say that engineers must “hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public” were compromised when cost and competition overshadowed safety considerations.


---

## Regulatory Shortcomings and Conflicts of Interest

The FAA’s decision to delegate significant portions of the 737 MAX’s certification to Boeing itself created conditions for conflict of interest. While delegation can improve efficiency, Boeing employees were placed in the position of evaluating their own company’s work.

The ASME Canons clearly emphasize objectivity and do not tolerate deceptive acts, yet internal safety inspectors were incentivized to prioritize rapid certification over thorough scrutiny. The FAA, pressured by political and economic concerns about maintaining U.S. aerospace competitiveness, relied too heavily on Boeing’s assessments. This diminished the regulatory independence necessary to ensure that potential hazards, such as MCAS’s single-sensor dependency, were appropriately disclosed and mitigated.


---

## Ethical Implications for Airlines and Pilots

Airlines purchasing the 737 MAX were told that pilots trained on earlier 737 models could operate the new aircraft with minimal additional instruction. This was marketed as a cost-saving advantage, but the ethical implications were substantial: Boeing knowingly omitted detailed information about MCAS from flight manuals to protect this marketing point. 

Customers entrusted their safety to airlines and manufacturers. Stakeholders, including families of victims, relied on engineers and executives to act with honesty and transparency. Instead, Boeing’s communications downplayed the risks and limited disclosure of MCAS’s capabilities and failure modes. This violated fundamental engineering ethical duties regarding truthfulness and avoidance of misleading statements.

---

## Internal Culture and Suppressed Engineering Concerns

Many Boeing engineers raised concerns about the pace of development, but internal emails later revealed a culture of pressure, dismissal of safety objections, and fear of retaliation. Ethical engineering culture requires that employees be empowered to voice safety concerns without professional jeopardy.

ASME Canons emphasize the responsibility to report unethical or unsafe practices, yet the environment at Boeing discouraged such actions.

When internal safety inspections are subordinate to production schedules or profit motives, engineers face conflicts between professional integrity and organizational loyalty. In the 737 MAX case, too many employees were pressured into compromise rather than supported in advocating for safe, transparent solutions.


---

## Investor Pressure and Executive Decision-Making

Investors and executives were driven by the desire to protect market share, maintain stock value, and minimize development costs. While financial health is essential for any engineering firm, ASME ethics principles highlight that the welfare of the public must outweigh commercial considerations. Boeing’s efforts to guarantee fast delivery and low costs for airlines ultimately created risks that jeopardized long-term trust, financial stability, and the company’s reputation.


---

## Transparency Failures After the First Crash

One of the most ethically troubling elements of the crisis was Boeing’s failure to disclose complete information about MCAS to the FAA, pilots, and the public. Many pilots did not even know the system existed. After the first crash, Boeing issued reassurances that MCAS was safe, even though internal documents suggested deeper concerns.


This violated several ASME Canons requiring honesty and full disclosure of risks. Engineers and organizations must communicate safety-critical information, even when disclosure could lead to financial loss or reputational harm. Ethical responsibility demands transparency because public trust depends on it.


---

## Conclusion

The Boeing 737 MAX crisis illustrates how ethical failures in engineering rarely stem from a single decision. Instead, they emerge from systemic issues: competitive pressure, flawed regulatory structures, internal cultural problems, and the prioritization of cost over safety.

Boeing executives, engineers, the FAA, airlines, investors, and regulators all played some role, whether through direct action or insufficient oversight. Applying the ASME Canons highlights that engineering ethics requires more than compliance with technical standards. There needs to be a demand for courage, honesty, and commitment to public safety.

Ultimately, the Boeing MCAS failure teaches that ethical engineering must place human welfare above all other considerations. Technological innovation cannot come at the cost of safety, and organizations must cultivate cultures that empower engineers to act in accordance with their professional and moral obligations.
